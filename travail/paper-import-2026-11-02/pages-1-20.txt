1.1.Related work.We first discuss earlier SAT and counting algorithms and the complexity
hypotheses contradicted by Theorem 1.1. We then compare our approach with earlier algebraic
algorithms and identify the existing constructions used in our proof, whose roles are developed in
the roadmap in Section 2.
1.1.1.SAT algorithms and the exponential-time hypotheses.Schöning’s local-search algorithm and
PPSZ are landmarks in the development of exponential-time algorithms fork-SAT [Sch99, PPSZ05].
Schöning’s randomized algorithm starts from a random assignment and repeatedly flips a uniformly
chosen variable in an unsatisfied clause. Its bounded-error decision time is
(n+m) O(1)(2−2/k) n
for each fixedk⩾ 3[ Sch99, Section 2]. In particular, this gives(n + m)O(1)(4/3)n time for3-SAT.
The PPSZ algorithm [PPSZ05] uses a different mechanism: bounded resolution derives additional
clauses, after which variables are assigned in a random order, taking forced values when available and
guessing otherwise. Fork = 3, 4, its original analysis gave stronger bounds for uniquely satisfiable
formulas than for general ones. The unique-SAT guarantees were later extended to general3-SAT
and4-SAT; for3-SAT this gave a randomizedO(1.308n)bound [Her14]. Hansen, Kaplan, Zamir, and
Zwick improved the PPSZ bound for every fixedk⩾ 3by biasing the algorithm’s guesses [HKZZ19].
Scheder obtained an improvement for the same range ofk by sharpening the analysis of the original
PPSZ algorithm [Sch24, Theorem 1.5]. For general3-SAT, a preprint by Jiang and Cai gives a
randomized O∗(1.307031578n)bound [JC26, Corollary 1.2], whereO∗ suppresses factors polynomial
in the input size. Liu’s deterministic algorithm combines branching with derandomized local search
and solves3-SAT inO(1.32793 n)time [Liu18, Theorem 21].
Randomized local search has also produced effective heuristics. The WalkSAT heuristic chooses an
unsatisfied clause and flips one of its variables, combining random choices with greedy moves [SKC94].
Experiments on formulas from circuit synthesis and diagnosis demonstrated the practical value of
this approach [SKC94, pp. 340–342, Tables 4–5].

Among uniformly random k-CNFs, instances at the satisfiability threshold were conjectured
to be the hardest for deciding satisfiability [VW21, Section 1]; for fixedk⩾ 3and lower clause
densities m/n⩽ 2k/kC, where C is a universal constant, polynomial-time almost-uniform sampling

and Williams [VW21, Theorem 3] give a PPZ-based algorithm that finds a solution in2n(1−Ω(logk/k))

The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) asserted that3-SAT requires2Ω(n) deterministic time
in the worst case, and hence so doesk-SAT for every fixedk⩾ 3[ IP01, Theorem 1]. The Strong
Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) made the sharper prediction thatk-SAT requires2 n(1−ok(1))
time as k grows. Precisely, for everyε∈ (0, 1), there is a fixedk for which no deterministic algorithm
solves k-SAT in2 (1−ε)n(n + m)O(1) time. The running-time bounds for Schöning’s algorithm and
PPSZ helped motivate this prediction: their improvements over exhaustive search diminish ask
grows [IP01, Open Problems, item 2].
The Gap Exponential Time Hypothesis (Gap-ETH) strengthens ETH by postulating constants
δ, γ >0for which noO(2γn)-time algorithm can distinguish a satisfiable3-CNF from one in which
every assignment violates at least aδ fraction of the clauses, even whenm = O(n)[ Din16, MR17].
The randomized version rules out an algorithm with the same running time that distinguishes
the two cases with probability at least2/3on every promised input [CCK+20, Conjecture 2.5].
It has been used to prove hardness of approximating Label Cover [Din16] and dense constraint
satisfaction problems [MR17]. It also rules out nontrivial fixed-parameter approximations for Clique
and Dominating Set. For Clique, the excluded ratios areo(OPT), where OPT is the maximum
clique size [CCK+20, Corollaries 5.3 and 5.6].
The Nondeterministic Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (NSETH) asserts that, for everyε >0,
some fixed k admits no nondeterministic2(1−ε)n(n + m)O(1)-time algorithm for deciding whether
a k-CNF isunsatisfiable, or equivalently whether ak-DNF is a tautology [CGI+16]. NSETH has
been used to establish barriers to deterministic fine-grained reductions from SAT to problems such
as 3SUM and All-Pairs Shortest Paths [CGI+16], and to delineate SETH-based lower bounds for
approximating directed graph diameter [Li21].
Theorem 1.1 refutes ETH, SETH, Gap-ETH, and NSETH. Indeed, testing whether the exact count
is positive or zero decides both satisfiability and unsatisfiability in deterministic2o(n)(n+m)Ok(1) time
for every fixedk; deciding satisfiability also solves the Gap-ETH promise problem. The deterministic
algorithm also refutes the bounded-error randomized versions of ETH [DHM+14, Section 1] and
SETH [CDL+16, Definition 2.1], and of Gap-ETH. It does not conflict with unconditional exponential
lower bounds for restricted proof systems such as regular resolution [BI13]. In 2016, Ryan Williams
refuted the Merlin–Arthur version of SETH (MA-SETH) by giving a protocol that certifiesk-CNF
unsatisfiability in2 n/2 poly(n, m)time [Wil16, Theorem 3.3].

1.1.3.Implications for fine-grained complexity.ETH and SETH became standard assumptions for
parameterized and fine-grained complexity [IPZ01, VW19]. ETH-based reductions yielded lower
bounds for the dependence on solution size in Clique [CHKX06], and on treewidth in constraint
satisfaction and partitioned subgraph isomorphism [Mar10]. SETH supported sharp exponential
bounds for Hitting Set and Set Splitting [CDL+16], and for Independent Set and Dominating Set
parameterized by treewidth [LMS18]. It also gave bounds on possible speedups of pseudopolynomial
algorithms for Subset Sum and Bicriteria Path [ABHS19].
For problems inP, SETH was used to rule out polynomial-factor speedups over quadratic running
times for edit distance [BI18], longest common subsequence and dynamic time warping [BK15],
Fréchet distance [Bri14], and regular-expression matching [BI16]. Other applications concerned
approximating graph diameter [RV13] and maintaining reachability counts or the size of the largest
strongly connected component under graph updates [AW14]. Together these results made SETH a
common basis for explaining the apparent optimality of algorithms across quite different problems.
Our refutation of ETH and SETH removes one basis for the hardness claims above. Some
of the same lower bounds follow from weaker hypotheses that may still hold. For example, the
lower bounds for longest common subsequence and regular-expression matching follow from the
Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture (OVC) [BK15, BI16]. OVC asserts that for every fixedε >0, no
O(N 2−ε poly(d))-time algorithm can decide whether two sets ofN Boolean vectors of dimension
d contain an orthogonal pair, one vector from each set. The reduction from SAT to Orthogonal
Vectors shows that SETH implies OVC [Wil05, VW19]. The lower bounds for Fréchet distance,
graph diameter approximation, and the treewidth dependence of Independent Set and Dominating
Set also follow from the hypothesis that unrestricted CNF-SAT admits no2(1−δ)n poly(n, m)-time
algorithm for any constantδ >0[ Bri14, RV13, LMS18]. Theorem 1.1 gives a running-time guarantee
for each fixed clause widthk, while this hypothesis allows the width to grow withn.
To obtain a faster algorithm for a target problem from our SAT algorithm, we need a reduction in
the other direction, from that problem to fixed-width SAT, that controls the number of variables.
Set Splitting with sets of size at mostk has such a reduction [CDL+16, Observation 3.6]: use one
Boolean variable per element to specify its side of the split. For each set, one clause requires at least
one of its variables to be true, and a second requires at least one to be false. The resultingk-CNF
has one variable per universe element, so for fixedk Theorem 1.1 gives subexponential time in the
universe size, up to a polynomial input-size factor.
1.1.4.Prior work on algebraic algorithms for combinatorial problems.Our algorithm is algebraic,
based on polynomial interpolation of carefully chosen auxiliary variables. Below we discuss prior work
on using algebraic methods to design algorithm for combinatorial problems. For broader background,
see the surveys of Koutis and Williams [KW16] on algebraic fingerprints, Williams [Wil14] on the
polynomial method in algorithm design, and Husfeldt [Hus11] on algorithmic inclusion–exclusion.
Williams’s split-and-list algorithm [Wil05] partitions the variables of a2-CSP into three groups,
enumerates partial assignments, and combines them using fast matrix multiplication. Koivisto [Koi06]
gives a direct sum–product formulation. The algorithm of Sections 3–6 likewise enumerates as-
signments within groups. Fixing the shared measurements⃗ ymakes their contributions multiply
independently. Scott and Sorkin [SS09, Section 6] extend split-and-list to polynomial-valued weights
and arbitrary rings. Our summation routine similarly uses truncated polynomial rings to retain
several coefficients in one computation.
Björklund [Bjö16, Sections 2–3] uses inclusion–exclusion and polynomial interpolation to reduce
permanent and directed Hamiltonian-cycle counting to a weighted sum over smaller instances. He
adds the weights of identical instances in a table, then solves each distinct instance with nonzero
total weight once. Björklund, Kaski, and Williams [BKW19] use low-degree curves and generalized

1.1.5.Antecedents of the matrix construction.Our counting algorithm evaluates sums of products
of polynomials over Boolean assignments. The polynomials share variables, so these sums do not
factor into independent smaller sums. We introduce linear substitutions, depending polynomially on
an interpolation parameter, that make such a factorization possible after fixing a few integer linear
measurements. An auxiliary square rational matrixM0 underlies these substitutions: a bound on
rank(I−M 0)limits the number of measurements, while restrictions on the nonzero entries ofM0
keep the substitution degrees small. Section 2.7 explains this connection.
Related interpolation methods have been used to obtain low-rank approximations to structured
matrices. In [AW17, Section 3], a low-degree polynomial matches(−1)j on an interval of integer
inner products j, using the interpolation lemma of [AW15, Lemma 3.1]. This gives low-rank
matrices agreeing with Walsh–Hadamard matrices in most entries, a result in the study of matrix
rigidity [Val77]. Related interpolation gives rigidity bounds for Kronecker powers in [AGP23,
Theorem 6.1]. Dvir and Liu [DL20, Claim 2.20 and Lemmas 3.3–3.4] use a spectral approach: sparse
changes to a function invariant under permutations of its coordinates make many Fourier coefficients
vanish, reducing the rank of its group-circulant matrix.
Our construction also interpolates( −1)j on a short interval, now to obtain a polynomial in
commuting permutation matrices. The same polynomial gives both the rank bound forI−M 0
and the restrictions on the nonzero entries ofM0. We prove these properties and their quantitative
bounds directly in Section 4.
1.1.6.Sparsification and parity tests.We use the counting-preserving version [DHM+14, Appendix A]
of the sparsification lemma [IPZ01], with quantitative bounds from [CIP06, Section 6] and [Cal09,
Lemma 5.14 and equation (5.4)]. This partitions the satisfying assignments among sparse formulas
on the same Boolean variables. Appendix A records the disjoint branching argument and the bounds
needed when its parameter varies with the input size.
The initial parity tests are the constraints of a Tanner code [Tan81, Section II], applied to the
binary vector recording which clauses an assignment violates. We use the edge-based expander-code
construction of [SS96, Sections IV and VI]: each graph edge carries one bit, and each vertex requires
its incident bits to belong to a fixed binary linear code. The local code’s minimum distance, together
with graph expansion, forces every nonzero vector satisfying all these constraints to have large
support. This construction and distance argument are standard. Lemma 6.1 supplies the parameter
choices needed here, including the bounds on the number and support size of the tests, and constructs
the graph and local code by deterministic exhaustive searches. The graph is the bipartite double
